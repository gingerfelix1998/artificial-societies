# Waltz 1981 — More May Be Better

## Argument

Waltz argues that the gradual spread of nuclear weapons is more likely to promote peace than to
undermine it, and the argument matters here less for its proliferation conclusion than for the
theory of crisis behaviour it rests on.

The foundation is that nuclear weapons make the costs of war so clear and so certain that
miscalculation becomes very difficult. Conventional war has historically been possible because
leaders could believe victory was achievable at acceptable cost; nuclear weapons remove that
belief. States possessing them therefore become cautious, and caution is what deterrence
requires. He notes that the required force is small — second-strike survivability against cities
is achievable without parity or superiority — which means the demanding conditions the American
literature specified are not in fact necessary.

He is correspondingly dismissive of the arguments that new nuclear states will be reckless, that
their command systems will be inadequate, or that pre-emptive incentives will be acute. New
nuclear powers, he argues, behave as existing ones did: they learn caution quickly because the
consequences of error are unambiguous, and their weapons need only be survivable enough to make
an attacker uncertain.

The crisis-relevant core is therefore a claim about rationality under nuclear conditions: leaders
facing nuclear risk behave more prudently than they would otherwise, and structural pressure does
the work regardless of regime type or organisational competence. This is the direct antithesis of
Sagan's position, and the two published their disagreement jointly as *The Spread of Nuclear
Weapons: A Debate* — which makes them the field's cleanest documented opposition and the natural
test of whether a corpus-grounded panel produces genuine divergence.

## Claims as stated

- The gradual spread of nuclear weapons is more likely to promote peace than to undermine it.
- Nuclear weapons make the costs of war clear and certain, so miscalculation becomes difficult.
- Conventional war was possible because leaders could believe victory was achievable.
- States possessing nuclear weapons become cautious, and caution is what deterrence requires.
- The force required for deterrence is small; parity and superiority are unnecessary.
- New nuclear states learn caution quickly because the consequences of error are unambiguous.
- Weapons need only be survivable enough to make an attacker uncertain.
- Structural pressure produces prudence regardless of regime type or organisational competence.

## Verify

This is the Adelphi Paper; confirm number and year. The claim that small forces suffice is the
load-bearing one and should be taken from the text. Note the direct opposition to Sagan, published
jointly in later editions of the debate volume; the Waltz–Sagan disagreement on this dimension is
the field's clearest documented divergence and the best available discriminant-validity test.
