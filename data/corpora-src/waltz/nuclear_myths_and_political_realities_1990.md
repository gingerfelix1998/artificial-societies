# Waltz 1990 — Nuclear Myths and Political Realities

## Argument

This article is Waltz's most direct engagement with American nuclear strategy, and it attacks the
same target Jervis attacks from a structural rather than a psychological direction. Its claim is
that most of what the strategic community believed about nuclear weapons was myth generated by
carrying conventional-war reasoning into a domain where it does not apply.

The myths he identifies concern the relevance of numbers, of counterforce capability, and of
war-fighting doctrine. If deterrence requires only that an adversary be uncertain of destroying
one's retaliatory force, then the size of arsenals above that threshold is strategically
irrelevant, arms races are politically rather than strategically driven, and counterforce
targeting purchases nothing because it cannot deliver a disarming strike. Damage-limitation
capability and escalation-dominance postures are likewise pursuing military advantages that
nuclear conditions do not permit.

The positive claim is that deterrence is easy and robust rather than delicate and demanding. What
deters is the prospect of destruction, not a calculated balance of forces, and the prospect
requires only a small survivable capability. Wohlstetter's delicate balance, on this reading, is
a category error: it applies conventional force-ratio logic to a situation where ratios do not
determine outcomes.

For 1962 injection the effect is to tell the panel that the strategic anxieties structuring its
deliberation are misconceived — that both the missile-gap concern and McNamara's counterforce
programme rest on the same conventional-war reasoning. It also sits in direct opposition to
Wohlstetter, who is on the pre-cutoff panel, making this an unusually clean removal-and-injection
pairing on a single dimension.

## Claims as stated

- Most beliefs about nuclear strategy are myths produced by conventional-war reasoning.
- Deterrence requires only that an adversary be uncertain of destroying one's retaliatory force.
- Arsenal size above that threshold is strategically irrelevant.
- Arms races are politically rather than strategically driven.
- Counterforce targeting cannot deliver a disarming strike and therefore purchases nothing.
- Damage limitation and escalation dominance pursue advantages nuclear conditions do not permit.
- What deters is the prospect of destruction, not a calculated balance of forces.
- Deterrence is easy and robust rather than delicate and demanding.
- Applying force-ratio logic to nuclear situations is a category error.
