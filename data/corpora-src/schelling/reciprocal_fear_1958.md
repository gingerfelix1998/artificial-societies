# Schelling 1958 — The Reciprocal Fear of Surprise Attack

## Argument

This short paper isolates a mechanism that is analytically separable from everything else
in the deterrence literature, and it is the reason to hold it as a document distinct from
*The Strategy of Conflict* even though it later appeared there as a chapter. Its subject is
how war can occur between two parties neither of whom wants it.

The mechanism is recursive. Suppose neither side intends to attack, but each knows that
striking first confers an advantage — because the opponent's retaliatory force is
vulnerable, so a first strike reduces the damage one suffers. Each side then has to consider
not only the opponent's intentions but the opponent's fear about one's own intentions. If A
believes B might strike first out of fear, A has an incentive to strike first to avoid being
struck. But B, reasoning about A's reasoning, acquires the same incentive. The fear is
reciprocal and self-amplifying, and at no point does either party need to want war for the
process to arrive at war.

The critical property Schelling identifies is that this is independent of aggressive
intent. It is not a model of an aggressor deterred or not deterred; it is a model in which
both parties are defensive and the structure of the situation generates the attack. That
makes it a different kind of danger from the one deterrence theory is usually addressed to,
and it cannot be solved by strengthening resolve or clarifying threats — those measures may
worsen it, because a more resolute opponent is a more frightening one.

The remedy the argument implies is structural rather than declaratory: reduce the advantage
of striking first. If neither side gains from going first, the recursion has nothing to feed
on. This is the intellectual foundation of arms control understood as stabilisation rather
than as disarmament — the idea that adversaries with no intention of cooperating on anything
else may still share an interest in making surprise attack unprofitable. That reframing is
one of Schelling's most durable contributions and it followed from this paper.

Two consequences bear directly on a crisis scenario. First, an adversary action that
increases one's own vulnerability, or that appears to prepare a first strike, is dangerous
even if it is defensively motivated — and the observing side generally cannot tell which it
is. Second, one's own precautionary measures are observed by the adversary and feed their
side of the recursion, so protective steps are not strategically neutral. A Schelling
persona built on this paper should reason about what an action *looks like to the other
side*, and should treat the adversary's fear as a variable to be managed rather than as
their problem.

The paper also anticipates, in a specific form, what Jervis would later develop at length
as the spiral model. That overlap matters for the ablation design: the mechanism is
partially present in 1962 in Schelling's version, so injecting Jervis's 1976 treatment is
not adding a wholly absent idea but adding a fuller and more psychologically grounded one.
Any arm claiming to inject "misperception" needs to say what is already there.

## Claims as stated

- War can occur between parties neither of whom wants it
- A first-strike advantage gives each side an incentive to pre-empt
- Each side must reason about the other's fear of its own intentions, not only about the
  other's intentions
- The resulting fear is reciprocal and self-amplifying
- The mechanism operates independently of aggressive intent; both parties may be defensive
- Strengthening resolve or clarifying threats may worsen rather than improve the situation
- The remedy is to reduce the advantage of striking first
- Adversaries who cooperate on nothing else may share an interest in making surprise attack
  unprofitable
- An adversary's defensive preparations are indistinguishable from attack preparations to
  the observer
- One's own precautionary measures feed the adversary's side of the recursion
