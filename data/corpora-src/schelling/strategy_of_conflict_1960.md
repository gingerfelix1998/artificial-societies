# Schelling 1960 — The Strategy of Conflict

## Argument

Schelling's book reframes conflict as bargaining, and that reframing is its contribution.
Its subject is not war but the class of situations in which parties' interests are partly
opposed and partly shared — where each would prefer to prevail but both would prefer to
avoid the worst outcome. Nuclear confrontation is the leading case, but the analysis is
general and much of the book's evidence comes from labour disputes, blackmail, traffic and
ordinary haggling.

The central and most counter-intuitive result concerns commitment. In a bargaining
situation, the ability to *restrict one's own options* can be a source of strength. A party
who can credibly show that they cannot retreat — because retreat has been made impossible,
publicly foreclosed, or delegated away — shifts the burden of avoiding disaster onto the
opponent. Freedom of action, ordinarily an asset, becomes a liability, because an opponent
who knows you can still concede will wait for you to do so. This is why the strategic move
is often to give something up rather than to acquire something.

The corollary is that threats have a credibility problem the book takes as its main
analytical difficulty. A threat that would be irrational to execute does not bind, and both
parties know it. Schelling's answer is not to make the irrational threat sound sincere but
to change its structure: convert a decision one would not take into a risk one does not
fully control. This is the *threat that leaves something to chance* — instead of promising
to do something disastrous, one takes an action that raises the probability of disaster
occurring through processes neither side commands. The threat becomes credible because
executing it is no longer a choice.

A second major theme is tacit bargaining and the role of focal points. Where explicit
communication is impossible or untrustworthy, outcomes converge on solutions that are
salient — obvious, precedented, or possessing some conspicuous quality — rather than on
solutions that are optimal. This matters for limited war, where the limits that hold are the
ones both sides can recognise without negotiating, which tend to be qualitative and stark
(a river, a border, the nuclear threshold itself) rather than quantitative and graduated.
Once a salient limit is crossed, no equally obvious replacement exists, which is why
thresholds are more stable than gradations.

He also distinguishes deterrence from compellence, though the terminology is developed more
fully later: deterring an opponent from acting is structurally easier than compelling them
to act, because the former requires only that one hold position while the latter requires
the opponent to move first and visibly.

For a 1962 crisis panel this is the most directly applicable pre-cutoff text, because it is
about the management of a confrontation rather than about force structure. A Schelling
persona should reason about what an action communicates, about whether a commitment can be
made visible and irreversible, about which limits are salient enough to hold, and about
manipulating shared risk rather than issuing threats. It is also the text most likely to
recommend an action that deliberately *reduces* one's own flexibility, which is a
recommendation no other persona in the panel will generate.

Note that *Arms and Influence* (1966) develops the coercive-bargaining and risk-manipulation
material considerably further and is **not** available in 1962. As with Kahn, this gives an
unusually clean injection arm: the same author's later development, controlling for
authorial voice.

## Claims as stated

- Conflict is bargaining; most situations are mixed-motive rather than purely opposed
- Restricting one's own options can strengthen one's bargaining position
- Freedom of action is a liability when the opponent knows concession remains available
- A threat that would be irrational to execute does not bind
- Credibility can be manufactured by converting a decision into a risk one does not fully
  control
- Raising shared risk is more credible than promising a self-destructive action
- Where communication is absent or untrusted, outcomes converge on salient rather than
  optimal solutions
- Limits that hold in war are those both sides recognise without negotiation
- Qualitative thresholds are more stable than quantitative gradations
- Once a salient limit is crossed, no equally obvious replacement exists
- Deterring is structurally easier than compelling

## Verify

The commitment and leave-something-to-chance arguments are the load-bearing ones and should
be checked against the text; the latter's development between this book and *Arms and
Influence* determines what is legitimately available in 1962 and is central to the ablation
design. Confirm whether the deterrence/compellence distinction is named as such here or
only later. Check which chapters are reprinted RAND papers, since those have their own
dates and one — the reciprocal fear paper — is held as a separate document.
