# Schelling 1966 — Arms and Influence

## Argument

Where *The Strategy of Conflict* built a general theory of bargaining and applied it to military
situations, this book is about military force specifically, and its organising claim is that the
capacity to inflict pain is a form of diplomacy rather than an alternative to it.

Schelling separates two uses of force that had been conflated. Brute force takes what it wants
by defeating the opponent's capacity to resist. Coercion obtains compliance by threatening harm
the opponent can avoid by complying, which requires that the opponent retain the ability to
choose and therefore that the harm be withheld. These have opposite requirements: brute force
needs the opponent disarmed, coercion needs the opponent capable of deciding.

The nuclear consequence is the book's central observation. Historically the ability to hurt a
country's population came after defeating its army; nuclear weapons made pain available without
victory first. That inverts the traditional sequence and makes the power to hurt directly usable
as bargaining leverage in crisis rather than as a consequence of winning.

He formalises the deterrence–compellence distinction that 1960 had gestured at. Deterrence asks
the opponent not to act, can be satisfied by inaction, has no deadline, and leaves the first move
to the challenger. Compellence asks the opponent to act, requires a deadline, and demands a
visible concession — which is harder, because complying is publicly humiliating in a way that
merely refraining is not. The risk-manipulation material becomes a full treatment: since a threat
of self-destructive action is not credible, the credible move is to create a shared risk neither
party fully controls and then decline to reduce it, making the competition one in resolve to
tolerate danger rather than willingness to execute a decision.

## Claims as stated

- The capacity to inflict harm is a form of diplomacy, not an alternative to it.
- Brute force defeats resistance; coercion obtains compliance and requires the opponent retain
  the ability to choose.
- Coercion works by withholding harm, so the harm must remain available and unused.
- Nuclear weapons made the power to hurt available without first achieving military victory.
- This inverts the traditional sequence in which pain followed defeat.
- Deterrence asks for inaction, has no deadline, and leaves the first move to the challenger.
- Compellence asks for action, requires a deadline, and demands a visible concession.
- Compellence is structurally harder than deterrence because complying is publicly humiliating.
- A threat of self-destructive action is not credible; a shared uncontrolled risk can be.
- Crisis competition is a competition in tolerance of risk rather than willingness to act.
- Tacit understandings form around salience without negotiation.

## Verify

The coercion–brute-force and deterrence–compellence distinctions are the load-bearing frameworks
and should be taken from the text with their actual definitions. Establish precisely how much of
the risk-manipulation argument is new here versus present in 1960 — the ablation design depends
on that boundary, and it is the most important verification task in this document.
