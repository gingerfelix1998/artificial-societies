# Wohlstetter 1974 — Is There a Strategic Arms Race?

## Argument

Fifteen years after "The Delicate Balance of Terror", Wohlstetter attacks a claim that had become
orthodoxy in the arms-control community: that the superpowers were locked in an action–reaction
arms race in which each side's build-up drove the other's.

His method is empirical and deflationary. Examining the actual record of American strategic force
levels against Soviet ones, he argues the numbers do not show the pattern the theory predicts.
American forces did not track Soviet increases; over substantial periods the United States
reduced or held its strategic delivery vehicles while Soviet numbers rose. If the arms-race model
were right, American decisions should correlate with Soviet ones, and the correlation is weak or
absent.

He also attacks the forecasting record. Predictions of American build-ups, made by arms-control
advocates in support of the action–reaction thesis, had consistently overshot what the United
States actually built. A model whose predictions fail systematically in one direction is not
merely imprecise; it is misspecified.

The positive implication is that strategic force decisions are driven by domestic budgetary,
technological and organisational factors more than by adversary behaviour — a conclusion which,
awkwardly for the received reading of Wohlstetter as a hawk, undercuts the argument that American
restraint would elicit Soviet restraint *and* the argument that Soviet build-ups compel American
responses.

This is the most useful document for correcting a caricatured Wohlstetter persona. His position
is that vulnerability must be attended to continuously, not that arsenals must be maximised, and
here he argues explicitly against the mechanism usually attributed to hawkish positions.

## Claims as stated

- The action–reaction arms race model is not supported by the historical force-level record.
- American strategic force decisions do not track Soviet increases.
- Over substantial periods the United States reduced or held delivery vehicles while Soviet
  numbers rose.
- Predictions of American build-ups made in support of the arms-race thesis consistently overshot.
- Systematic one-directional forecast error indicates a misspecified model, not imprecision.
- Force decisions are driven more by domestic budgetary, technological and organisational factors
  than by adversary behaviour.
- It follows that unilateral restraint will not reliably elicit reciprocal restraint.

## Verify

Confirm the venue and year. The force-level evidence is the load-bearing element and the specific
series he uses should be checked, since the claim depends on which measure of strategic capability
is counted. Confirm the final implication, which is inference from the argument rather than
necessarily stated.
