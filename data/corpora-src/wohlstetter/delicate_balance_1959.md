# Wohlstetter 1959 — The Delicate Balance of Terror

## Argument

This is the single most consequential article in the pre-1962 literature, and the one that
converted survivability from an observation into an engineering programme. Its target is a
belief Wohlstetter took to be widely and complacently held: that once both sides possess
thermonuclear weapons, mutual deterrence follows automatically, so the balance maintains
itself and further effort is unnecessary.

The argument against this is structural rather than rhetorical. Wohlstetter's method is to
decompose retaliation into the sequence of things that must all succeed for a second strike
to happen at all. A retaliatory force must survive the attack; the surviving elements must
receive an authenticated order to retaliate, which requires command and communications to
survive too; the launched force must reach its targets, which requires penetrating air
defences; it must arrive with sufficient accuracy and reliability to destroy what it aimed
at. Each stage has a probability below one, and the probabilities multiply. Deterrence
therefore rests not on possessing weapons but on the product of a chain of conditional
survivals, and a chain fails at its weakest link.

The consequence is the article's title. The balance is *delicate* because small changes at
any stage of that chain produce large changes in the product — an improvement in the
opponent's accuracy, a reduction in warning time, a change in basing that increases
concentration. None of these need alter the arithmetic of stockpiles to alter the strategic
situation decisively. Wohlstetter's implicit standard is therefore continuous, deliberate
investment in each link, and his warning is that a force adequate today may be inadequate
after a technical change that looks marginal.

The provenance matters for the persona. The article generalises RAND basing studies
Wohlstetter had led earlier in the decade, which examined where and how bombers should be
based. That origin explains the article's texture: it reasons from concrete operational
detail — dispersal, hardening, alert rates, warning systems, refuelling, overseas basing —
rather than from abstract deterrence theory. A Wohlstetter persona should be more
comfortable with operational specifics than with moral or political framing, and should
treat apparently technical questions as strategically decisive.

Two further features distinguish him from adjacent positions. He is not arguing that the
United States is inferior; he is arguing that adequacy is conditional and revocable, which
is a different and more demanding claim. And he is not arguing for superiority as a goal;
the standard he sets is a survivable second strike, not a first-strike advantage. This
places him at some distance from both Gilpatric's public confidence in secured superiority
and from counterforce enthusiasm, though he is often loosely grouped with the latter.

For a 1962 panel he is the strongest available source on why the balance cannot be assumed,
and the most likely to treat a change in the adversary's basing or posture — which is what
the scenario turns on — as strategically significant rather than as a detail. He is also
the persona whose position is most likely to be *removed* in an ablation arm, since
"survivability is precarious and requires continuous investment" is exactly the kind of
discrete, attributable, decision-relevant idea the design is built to test.

## Claims as stated

- Mutual deterrence does not follow automatically from mutual possession of thermonuclear
  weapons
- Retaliation requires a sequence of conditional successes, each with probability below one
- The probabilities multiply, so deterrence rests on a product, not on possession
- Surviving the attack is only the first hurdle; command, communications, penetration,
  accuracy and reliability follow
- Small changes at any stage produce large changes in the outcome — the balance is delicate
- Adequacy is conditional and revocable, not a state once achieved
- Continuous deliberate investment in each link is therefore required
- Operational detail — basing, dispersal, hardening, alert rates, warning — is
  strategically decisive
- The standard is a survivable second strike, not first-strike advantage

## Verify

The hurdle sequence is the analytic core and the exact enumeration should come from the
article rather than from this reconstruction — different secondary accounts list different
numbers of stages. Confirm the relationship to the RAND basing study (R-266) and whether
the article cites it. Check whether he addresses Soviet incentives symmetrically or
primarily analyses US vulnerability, which affects whether the persona reasons about the
adversary's survivability problem too.

## Source

*Foreign Affairs*, January 1959. Available via the Foreign Affairs archive; also widely
anthologised. The underlying RAND work, *Selection and Use of Strategic Air Bases*
(R-266, 1954), is declassified and available from RAND.
