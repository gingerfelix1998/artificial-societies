# Freedman 1981 — The Evolution of Nuclear Strategy

## Argument

The standard history of nuclear strategic thought, and its value in a panel is unusual: it is a
work *about* the corpus rather than a contribution to it, which makes it the one document that can
supply a persona with an account of how the other positions relate to each other.

Freedman's organising argument is that nuclear strategy developed less as a cumulative
intellectual advance than as a sequence of attempts to solve a problem that has no solution. The
problem is that nuclear weapons are extremely effective at deterring and almost useless for
anything else, so any doctrine seeking to make them usable — massive retaliation, limited nuclear
options, counterforce, flexible response, damage limitation — runs into the same wall from a
different direction. The apparent progression of doctrines is therefore better read as a series of
rediscoveries than as refinement.

He is particularly sharp on the relationship between strategic theory and policy. Civilian
strategists at RAND and elsewhere achieved genuine intellectual influence, but the doctrines
adopted were shaped as much by budget politics, service interests and alliance management as by
analysis, and the published theory often rationalised decisions taken for other reasons. His
account of the McNamara period is accordingly less admiring than the participants' own.

The book's most quoted conclusion is that nuclear strategy is, in the end, about the management of
a relationship rather than the design of an operation — that the object is to sustain a
condition of mutual restraint rather than to prepare an outcome.

For a 1962 panel this is a distinctive injection: it does not add a position on a dimension but
adds a meta-position, that the search for usable options is itself the error. It overlaps Jervis
1984 on that conclusion while reaching it historically rather than logically.

## Claims as stated

- Nuclear strategy developed as repeated attempts to solve a problem with no solution.
- Nuclear weapons are effective at deterring and almost useless for other purposes.
- Every doctrine seeking to make them usable meets the same obstacle from a different direction.
- The apparent progression of doctrines is a series of rediscoveries rather than refinement.
- Adopted doctrine was shaped by budget politics, service interests and alliance management.
- Published theory often rationalised decisions taken for other reasons.
- Nuclear strategy concerns the management of a relationship rather than the design of an
  operation.
- The object is to sustain mutual restraint rather than to prepare an outcome.

## Verify

Later editions extend the narrative considerably; use the 1981 text for 1981 claims and do not
attribute post-Cold-War material to it. The characterisation of the McNamara period should be
checked directly. As a historiographical work its claims are about other authors, so citations
from it should not be mistaken for those authors' own positions.

## Source

Freedman, *The Evolution of Nuclear Strategy* (Macmillan/IISS, 1981). Third edition 2003 and a
2019 edition with Jeffrey Michaels add substantial material. Manifest only.
