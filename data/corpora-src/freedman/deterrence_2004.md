# Freedman 2004 — Deterrence

## Argument

A short conceptual treatment written after the Cold War, and useful for a panel because it
disaggregates a term the earlier literature used as a single thing.

Freedman's central distinction is between deterrence by punishment — threatening costs the
adversary wishes to avoid — and deterrence by denial, persuading the adversary that the action
will not succeed. The Cold War literature concentrated almost entirely on punishment because
nuclear weapons made it available and denial impossible against nuclear attack, but denial is
historically the more common mechanism and the more reliable where it is achievable.

He adds a second distinction between internalised and non-internalised deterrence. Where a
prohibition has been internalised, the deterred party is not weighing costs at all, and the
deterrer need do nothing to sustain the effect. Where it has not, deterrence requires continuous
maintenance of a credible threat. This bears directly on the taboo–tradition question and gives
it a general framing.

His third theme is the limits of the concept. Deterrence requires an adversary who can be
identified, who calculates, and who has something to lose — conditions that hold for states and
often fail elsewhere. Extending the term to cover every attempt at dissuasion, he argues, empties
it.

For a 1962 panel this is a moderate injection. The punishment/denial distinction reframes the
blockade question, since a quarantine is denial rather than punishment and the literature the
panel holds is almost entirely about punishment.

## Claims as stated

- Deterrence by punishment threatens costs; deterrence by denial persuades that the action will
  fail.
- Cold War theory concentrated on punishment because nuclear weapons made it available.
- Denial is historically more common and more reliable where achievable.
- Internalised deterrence requires no maintenance; non-internalised deterrence requires a
  continuously credible threat.
- Deterrence requires an adversary who can be identified, who calculates, and who has something to
  lose.
- Extending the term to cover all dissuasion empties it of content.

## Verify

Confirm publisher and year — this appears in a Polity conceptual series and the edition matters.
The punishment/denial distinction originates with Glenn Snyder (1961); Freedman develops rather
than originates it, and Snyder's version is pre-cutoff, which affects whether this counts as an
injection at all.
