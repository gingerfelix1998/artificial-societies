# Jervis 1984 — The Illogic of American Nuclear Strategy

## Argument

A direct attack on the doctrine the United States had pursued since the early 1960s, and its
target includes precisely the position McNamara held in 1962. Jervis's claim is that American
nuclear strategy is not merely mistaken in its choices but incoherent in its premises.

The premise he rejects is that nuclear war can be controlled. American doctrine had
progressively sought usable options: limited nuclear options, counterforce targeting,
damage-limitation capability, escalation dominance. All presuppose that once nuclear weapons are
used the exchange can be bounded, communicated about and terminated on terms. Jervis argues the
presupposition is unsupported. Command systems, time pressure, uncertainty about what has been
struck and by whom, and the organisational behaviour of forces under attack all argue against
control, and no amount of planning converts an uncontrollable process into a controllable one.

If that is right, mutual vulnerability is not a policy the United States adopted and could
abandon but a condition produced by the technology. The nuclear revolution consists in the fact
that neither side can escape the other's retaliation, and strategies seeking escape pursue
something unavailable. His diagnosis of why the strategic community kept trying is institutional:
military organisations require operational plans and cannot plan for a war they regard as
unfightable, so doctrine is generated by the need for doctrine rather than by strategic logic.

The consequences for crisis behaviour follow. If vulnerability is mutual and inescapable, risk
manipulation is the only real currency, and the search for advantage at particular rungs is
chasing a distinction without a difference. Counterforce superiority purchases nothing usable,
because a first strike cannot disarm and a limited strike cannot be kept limited.

## Claims as stated

- American nuclear strategy is incoherent in its premises, not merely mistaken in its choices.
- Limited nuclear options, counterforce and damage limitation all presuppose controllability.
- Nuclear exchange cannot be reliably controlled, bounded or terminated on terms.
- Command systems, time pressure and uncertainty under attack all argue against control.
- Mutual vulnerability is a condition produced by technology, not a policy that was chosen.
- Strategies seeking escape from mutual vulnerability pursue something unavailable.
- Military organisations generate doctrine because they require plans, not because the strategy
  is sound.
- Counterforce superiority purchases nothing usable.
- A first strike cannot disarm; a limited strike cannot be kept limited.
- Risk manipulation is the only real currency in a nuclear confrontation.
- Stability at the level of general war raises the risk of lower-level conflict.
