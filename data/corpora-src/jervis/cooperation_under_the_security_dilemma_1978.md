# Jervis 1978 — Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma

## Argument

This article formalises the mechanism the 1976 book described psychologically, and gives it two
variables that make it tractable. The security dilemma is the situation in which measures a state
takes to increase its own security decrease the security of others, so that both end less secure
having each acted defensively.

Jervis's contribution is that the severity of the dilemma is not constant. It depends on two
technological and doctrinal conditions. First, whether offensive and defensive weapons and
postures are distinguishable: where they are, a state can arm defensively without alarming
others, and intentions become readable from posture. Where they are not, every defensive
acquisition is indistinguishable from preparation for attack. Second, whether offence or defence
has the advantage: where defence is easier, a state can secure itself without threatening others
and the dilemma is mild; where offence is easier, security requires the capacity to attack and
the dilemma is acute.

Crossing these produces four worlds with sharply different prospects for cooperation, and the
most dangerous is one in which offence has the advantage and the two postures cannot be
distinguished — there, arms racing and preventive war become rational even between states with no
aggressive intent. The framework thus explains variation rather than asserting a constant
condition, which is what makes it usable as an analytic tool rather than a warning.

For nuclear questions the argument is uncomfortable: nuclear weapons are the extreme case of
offence-dominance and of indistinguishability, since the same missile serves retaliation and
first strike. That places the nuclear relationship in the worst of the four worlds unless
survivability can be established, which connects the article back to Wohlstetter's problem from
a different direction.

## Claims as stated

- Measures a state takes to increase its security can decrease the security of others.
- Both states can end less secure having each acted purely defensively.
- The severity of the dilemma varies with two conditions rather than being constant.
- The first condition is whether offensive and defensive postures are distinguishable.
- Where they are distinguishable, intentions can be read from posture.
- The second condition is whether offence or defence has the advantage.
- Where defence is easier, a state can secure itself without threatening others.
- The most dangerous world combines offence-dominance with indistinguishability.
- In that world, arms racing and preventive war are rational absent aggressive intent.
- Nuclear weapons are an extreme case of both offence-dominance and indistinguishability.

## Verify

The four-world typology and the two variables should be taken from the article with their actual
labels. Confirm how far Jervis applies the framework to nuclear weapons here as opposed to
conventional cases, since the nuclear application in the final paragraph above is partly
inference. The term itself is Herz's (1950); attribute the dilemma to Herz and the variable
framework to Jervis.
