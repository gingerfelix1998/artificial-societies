# Jervis 1976 — Perception and Misperception in International Politics

## Argument

Jervis imports cognitive psychology into international politics and argues that decision-makers
misread each other in patterned, predictable ways — not randomly, and not through stupidity, but
because of how people process ambiguous information under stress.

The organising contrast is between two models of adversary behaviour. The deterrence model holds
that conflict arises from aggression insufficiently opposed, so firmness prevents war and
concession invites it. The spiral model holds that conflict arises from mutual fear, so firmness
intensifies the other side's insecurity and produces the hostility it was meant to forestall.
Both are coherent, both have historical support, and the same evidence often fits both. A state
arming defensively looks identical to a state arming offensively. Choosing the wrong model is
therefore not a failure of analysis but an unavoidable risk, and the two prescribe opposite
responses.

He then catalogues specific errors. Decision-makers assume their own intentions are evident to
others while the other side's remain opaque, so they systematically underestimate how threatening
they appear. They read the adversary as more centralised and deliberate than it is, attributing
to strategy what is often bureaucratic accident. They fit new information to existing beliefs
rather than revising, and the more committed the belief the more the fitting. They over-apply
historical analogies, particularly the formative event of their own generation, drawn from a
single vivid case rather than a distribution.

A further mechanism concerns signals. Jervis distinguishes signals, whose meaning is
conventionally established, from indices, which carry information because they are costly or hard
to fake. Adversaries discount signals and attend to indices, while senders overrate the clarity
of their own signals. Carefully calibrated declaratory messages therefore routinely fail to
arrive as sent.

## Claims as stated

- Decision-makers misread each other in patterned, predictable ways.
- The deterrence and spiral models both fit much of the same evidence.
- The two models prescribe opposite responses to the same observation.
- A defensively arming state is observationally indistinguishable from an offensively arming one.
- Decision-makers assume their own intentions are evident while others' remain opaque.
- They therefore underestimate how threatening they appear.
- Adversaries are read as more centralised and deliberate than they are.
- Bureaucratic accident is misattributed to strategy.
- New information is fitted to existing beliefs rather than prompting revision.
- Historical analogies are over-applied and drawn from single vivid cases.
- Signals are conventional and discounted; indices are costly and attended to.
- Senders overrate the clarity of their own signalling.
- Defensive precautions increase the adversary's fear and therefore their incentive to move.

## Verify

The signal–index distinction is Jervis's and its definitions should come from the text. Confirm
whether the deterrence–spiral framing originates here or is drawn from earlier work he cites.
Note that the spiral mechanism is partially present in 1962 via Schelling's 1958 reciprocal-fear
paper; what Jervis adds is the psychological substrate and the explicit model dilemma. Any arm
described as injecting misperception must state what is already there.
