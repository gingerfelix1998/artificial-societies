# Posen 2003 — Command of the Commons

## Argument

**This summary is thin and the work has little bearing on a 1962 crisis scenario.**

Posen argues that the material basis of American primacy is command of the commons — the sea,
air above a certain altitude, and space — which the United States can use for military and
economic purposes while denying their use to others. Command is not omnipotence: it does not
extend to contested zones such as littorals, airspace within range of dense air defences, or
urban ground, where local actors can impose high costs.

The strategic conclusion is that American power is well suited to preventing others from
projecting force globally and poorly suited to controlling contested terrain, which implies a
restrained grand strategy exploiting the former and avoiding the latter.

For nuclear crisis purposes there is essentially nothing here: the argument is about post-Cold-War
conventional power projection and concerns capabilities that did not exist in 1962. Include only if
the design later adds a conventional-balance dimension.

## Claims as stated

- American primacy rests materially on command of the sea, high air and space.
- Command permits use while denying use to others.
- Command does not extend to littorals, dense air-defence airspace or urban ground.
- Contested zones allow local actors to impose disproportionate costs.
- The distribution of American advantage favours a restrained grand strategy.

## Verify

Everything above. Confirm the exact definition of the commons and the contested zones. Establish
whether any part bears on nuclear questions before including it in a panel corpus.
