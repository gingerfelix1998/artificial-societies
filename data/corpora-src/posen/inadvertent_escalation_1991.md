# Posen 1991 — Inadvertent Escalation

## Argument

Posen identifies a route to nuclear use that requires no decision to use nuclear weapons by
anyone. His subject is what happens when large conventional military organisations prosecute a
conventional war against a nuclear-armed adversary, and his finding is that the normal conduct of
such operations attacks the adversary's nuclear forces and command systems as a by-product.

The mechanism is operational rather than political. Military organisations pursue offensive
doctrines because offensives achieve their objectives and because their planning cultures favour
initiative. Those offensives are directed at targets defined by military utility — air defences,
command nodes, naval assets, transport — and in a nuclear-armed state those target sets overlap
heavily with the nuclear infrastructure. Attacking air defence to gain freedom of action degrades
early warning. Anti-submarine operations threaten ballistic-missile submarines, the most
survivable retaliatory force. Strikes on communications to disrupt conventional coordination also
disrupt nuclear command.

The victim cannot distinguish a conventional campaign from the opening phase of a disarming
strike, because the observable actions are the same. Its use-it-or-lose-it pressure therefore
rises, and rises precisely as its ability to assess the situation degrades. Neither side has
decided to escalate; the escalation is produced by two military organisations doing what they
were designed to do.

Political leadership is poorly positioned to prevent this. Operational detail is delegated by
necessity, the pace of a campaign outruns political review, and leaders typically do not know
which specific targets carry nuclear significance. The fog of war compounds it, and is worse for
the side being attacked.

## Claims as stated

- Conventional operations against a nuclear-armed state attack its nuclear forces and command as a
  by-product.
- Military organisations pursue offensive doctrines for organisational and doctrinal reasons.
- Conventional and nuclear target sets overlap heavily: air defence, command, naval assets.
- Degrading air defence degrades early warning.
- Anti-submarine operations threaten the most survivable retaliatory force.
- The victim cannot distinguish a conventional campaign from a disarming first strike.
- Use-it-or-lose-it pressure rises as assessment capability degrades.
- Escalation is produced by organisational interaction, not by a decision to escalate.
- Political leadership cannot readily prevent this: delegation is necessary, pace outruns review,
  and leaders do not know which targets carry nuclear significance.
- The fog of war is worse for the side under attack.

## Verify

The cases are NATO–Warsaw Pact scenarios in Central Europe and at sea; check how far the argument
is presented as general versus specific to that theatre before applying it to a 1962 Caribbean
scenario. Confirm the treatment of political control, which secondary summaries tend to state more
strongly than the text. The organisational premise overlaps Sagan and Jervis; the distinctively
Posen element is operational target overlap.
