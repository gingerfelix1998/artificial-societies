# Sagan 1993 — The Limits of Safety

## Argument

Sagan tests two competing organisational theories against the operational history of American
nuclear forces, and the result is the most damaging empirical finding in the nuclear-safety
literature.

High reliability theory holds that organisations can achieve extremely low failure rates through
redundancy, training, safety culture and clear authority. Normal accidents theory, from Perrow,
holds that systems combining interactive complexity with tight coupling produce serious accidents
as an inherent property, because unanticipated interactions occur faster than operators can
diagnose them. Sagan concludes the second better describes nuclear weapons operations.

Three findings do the work. The empirical record of near-misses is far worse than the public
record of zero accidental detonations suggests, so the safety record reflects good fortune as much
as good design, and inferring reliability from the absence of catastrophe is an error. Redundancy
can reduce safety rather than improve it, because added components add failure modes and
interaction paths and encourage operators to take risks they would avoid in a system they believed
fragile. And organisations pursue conflicting objectives — readiness competes with safety, and
under alert conditions readiness wins, so the periods of greatest danger are the periods of
greatest tension.

The Cuban Missile Crisis supplies a substantial part of his evidence. His account documents what
happened at the operational edge during the crisis: alert procedures executed in ways their
designers had not anticipated, warning systems generating false or ambiguous indications, and
individual officers making consequential decisions outside the intended chain.

## Claims as stated

- High reliability theory and normal accidents theory make competing predictions about nuclear
  operations.
- The evidence favours normal accidents theory.
- Interactive complexity combined with tight coupling produces serious accidents inherently.
- The near-miss record is far worse than the absence of detonations suggests.
- The safety record reflects good fortune as much as good design.
- Inferring reliability from the absence of catastrophe is an inferential error.
- Redundancy can reduce safety by adding failure modes and interaction paths.
- Redundancy encourages risk-taking operators would otherwise avoid.
- Organisations pursue conflicting objectives; readiness defeats safety under alert.
- The periods of greatest danger are the periods of greatest tension.
- Alert procedures are executed in ways their designers did not anticipate.

## Verify

Specific near-miss cases and their sourcing should come from the text, not from secondary
retellings, several of which overstate individual episodes. Confirm which crisis-period incidents
he documents as against those established by later scholarship. Confirm the redundancy argument's
scope, frequently overstated as general when the text is more careful. Contamination risk is at
its maximum here: the evidence is partly the near-misses of the crisis being simulated.
