# Sagan 1996 — Why Do States Build Nuclear Weapons?

## Argument

Sagan argues that the standard security explanation for proliferation is insufficient and offers
three competing models, each implying different non-proliferation policy.

The security model holds that states build weapons to counter external threats, particularly a
nuclear-armed adversary, and that proliferation therefore proceeds as a chain reaction. It is the
default in the field and, Sagan argues, over-applied: it fits some cases well and others badly,
and its apparent explanatory success partly reflects the ease of finding a threat retrospectively
for any state that built weapons.

The domestic politics model holds that weapons serve the interests of coalitions inside the state
— nuclear establishments, military services, politicians — which construct or amplify threat
narratives to secure programmes they want for other reasons. The norms model holds that weapons
are pursued for their symbolic value as markers of modernity and sovereignty, with acquisition or
restraint shaped by what a state's leaders believe a state like theirs should be.

The policy consequence is that the models diverge sharply. If security drives proliferation,
guarantees and threat reduction work. If domestic coalitions drive it, then the relevant lever is
domestic institutional structure. If norms drive it, then what matters is the prestige attached to
possession, which non-proliferation regimes may inadvertently increase.

For a 1962 crisis scenario this bears only obliquely — it concerns why states acquire weapons, not
how they behave in a confrontation. Its relevance to the panel is as background to Sagan's
organisational scepticism rather than as a crisis-decision input.

## Claims as stated

- The security model over-explains proliferation and fits some cases badly.
- Retrospective threat identification makes the security model appear more successful than it is.
- Domestic coalitions pursue weapons for interests other than security and construct threat
  narratives to justify them.
- Weapons carry symbolic value as markers of modernity and sovereignty.
- Norms shape both acquisition and restraint.
- The three models imply sharply different non-proliferation policies.
- Non-proliferation regimes may increase the prestige attached to possession.
