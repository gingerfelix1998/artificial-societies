# Blair 1993 — The Logic of Accidental Nuclear War

## Argument

Written with access to post-Soviet material unavailable for the 1985 book, this extends the
command-and-control argument into an account of how nuclear war could begin without either
government deciding on it.

The core claim is that both superpowers' operational postures had converged on launch-on-warning
in practice, whatever their declaratory policy said, because command fragility left no
alternative. That posture makes the decision to use nuclear weapons a function of warning-system
output within a window of minutes, and it distributes the effective decision across the sensors,
the assessment procedures and the officers reading them rather than concentrating it in political
authority. War becomes possible through the interaction of two such systems under alert.

Blair adds the alerting dynamic as a distinct mechanism. Raising alert levels is intended to
signal resolve and improve survivability, but it also loosens peacetime safeguards, disperses
weapons, shortens decision procedures and hands discretion downward. So the measures a
leadership takes to control a crisis reduce its control over the forces, and the two sides'
alerting interacts: each side's increase raises the other's readiness and further compresses both
sides' decision time.

The Soviet material lets him treat the asymmetry directly. He argues Soviet command arrangements
were more centralised and their warning coverage poorer, which produced a system more dependent
on prompt reaction and less able to ride out ambiguity — an unfavourable combination.

## Claims as stated

- Both superpowers' operational postures converged on launch-on-warning regardless of declaratory
  policy.
- Command fragility left no practical alternative to prompt reaction.
- The effective decision is distributed across sensors, procedures and duty officers.
- Nuclear war can begin through the interaction of two alerted systems without a government
  deciding on it.
- Raising alert levels loosens peacetime safeguards and hands discretion downward.
- Measures taken to control a crisis reduce control over the forces.
- Each side's alerting raises the other's readiness and compresses both sides' decision time.
- Soviet warning coverage was poorer and command more centralised, making the system more
  dependent on prompt reaction.
