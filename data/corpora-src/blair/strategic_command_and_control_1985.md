# Blair 1985 — Strategic Command and Control

## Argument

Blair relocates the vulnerability problem. The literature from Wohlstetter onward asked whether
retaliatory forces would survive a first strike. Blair, writing from experience as a Minuteman
launch officer, argues the forces were the easier problem and that the binding constraint is the
command, control and communications system that must survive alongside them and order their use.

The asymmetry is stark. Warheads can be hardened, dispersed, submerged and multiplied; the
command system cannot, because it is small, physically identifiable, and dependent on
communication links that are inherently fragile. A handful of successful strikes against command
nodes could sever the connection between political authority and the forces, leaving weapons
intact but unusable. Decapitation is therefore a cheaper route to disarming an opponent than
counterforce against the arsenal, and available to a much smaller attacking force.

The consequences are why this matters for a crisis. If the command system cannot be assumed to
survive, a leadership intending to retaliate must either delegate authority downward in advance,
so surviving commanders can act without instruction, or launch on warning, so the decision is
taken while the system still functions. Both respond to the same fragility and both are dangerous
in recognisable ways: delegation multiplies the number of people who could initiate use, and
launch-on-warning compresses the decision to minutes and makes it hostage to sensor reliability.
These are not perverse policy choices but the logical consequences of a vulnerability planners
could not eliminate.

He frames the underlying tension explicitly. Negative control — preventing unauthorised or
accidental use — and positive control — ensuring authorised use occurs — pull in opposite
directions. Safeguards making accidental launch harder also make retaliation less certain under
attack, and every arrangement is a chosen point on that trade-off rather than a solution to it.

## Claims as stated

- Command, control and communications survivability, not force survivability, is the binding
  constraint.
- Forces can be hardened, dispersed and multiplied; command systems largely cannot.
- Command nodes are few, identifiable and dependent on fragile links.
- Decapitation is a cheaper route to disarming an opponent than counterforce against the arsenal.
- A leadership that cannot assume its command system survives must delegate authority in advance
  or launch on warning.
- Both are logical consequences of command fragility rather than perverse choices.
- Delegation multiplies the number of actors who could initiate use.
- Launch-on-warning compresses decision time and makes it hostage to sensor reliability.
- Negative control and positive control pull in opposite directions.
- Every command arrangement is a chosen point on that trade-off, not a solution to it.
