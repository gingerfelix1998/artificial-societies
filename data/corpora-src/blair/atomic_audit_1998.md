# Schwartz ed. 1998 — Atomic Audit

## Argument

**This summary is thin and should be treated as a pointer.**

A collaborative Brookings accounting of the full cost of the American nuclear weapons programme
from 1940, to which Blair was a contributor rather than the lead editor. Its contribution is
fiscal and historical rather than strategic: it establishes that the programme's true cost,
including waste management, environmental remediation, dismantlement and secrecy, vastly exceeded
what appeared in defence budgets, and that much of it was never presented to Congress or the
public as a coherent total.

The strategic relevance is indirect. The argument that costs were systematically obscured
supports a wider claim about weak civilian oversight of nuclear programmes, which connects to
Blair's command-and-control work and to Sagan's organisational findings. But it does not bear on
crisis decision-making, and it has no plausible role in an injection arm for a 1962 scenario.

Recommendation: exclude from the panel corpus unless a fiscal or oversight dimension is added to
the design. If included, attribute to Schwartz as editor with Blair as contributor.

## Claims as stated

- The full cost of the US nuclear programme greatly exceeded appropriated defence budgets.
- Waste management, remediation, dismantlement and secrecy costs were largely uncounted.
- Costs were never presented as a coherent total to Congress or the public.
- Cost opacity is evidence of weak civilian oversight of nuclear programmes.
