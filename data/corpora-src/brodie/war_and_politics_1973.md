# Brodie 1973 — War and Politics

## Argument

Brodie's late work, and the fullest statement of the argument that had been implicit in
*Strategy in the Missile Age*: that strategy divorced from political purpose is not strategy.
Written after Vietnam, it is markedly more critical of the American strategic establishment,
including the analytic tradition Brodie had himself helped create.

The central claim is that military and civilian strategists alike had come to treat war as a
technical problem with technical solutions, and had thereby lost sight of the question war is
supposed to answer. He argues that decisions about force are political decisions throughout, not
political at the outset and technical thereafter, and that the professional separation of the two
had produced both the doctrinal incoherence of nuclear strategy and the specific failures of
Vietnam.

He is correspondingly hard on systems analysis and on the quantification of strategic questions,
arguing that the apparent rigour of the method concealed the arbitrariness of the assumptions fed
into it, and that quantitative confidence had displaced judgement about ends. This is a direct
criticism of the RAND method Wohlstetter exemplified and of the McNamara Pentagon's practice.

The other significant theme is the role of chance and of the incalculable in war, and the argument
that strategic theory systematically underweights both because they cannot be modelled.

For a 1962 panel this is a strong meta-level injection: it does not add a position on a strategic
dimension but attacks the standing of the analytic method that produced most of the panel's
positions. It sets late Brodie against early Brodie, which makes it the third available
same-author pairing alongside Kahn and Schelling.

## Claims as stated

- Strategy divorced from political purpose is not strategy.
- Decisions about force are political throughout, not political at the outset and technical after.
- Strategists had come to treat war as a technical problem with technical solutions.
- The professional separation of political and technical judgement produced doctrinal incoherence.
- Systems analysis conceals the arbitrariness of the assumptions fed into it.
- Quantitative confidence had displaced judgement about ends.
- Strategic theory systematically underweights chance and the incalculable because they resist
  modelling.
