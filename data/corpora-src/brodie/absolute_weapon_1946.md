# Brodie 1946 — The Absolute Weapon

## Argument

Brodie's contribution is the founding text of nuclear strategy, written within months of
Hiroshima and before either thermonuclear weapons or ballistic missiles existed. Its
achievement is to have identified, from a single year's evidence, the structural feature
that would organise the next four decades of argument: that a weapon against which no
defence is possible inverts the purpose of military force.

The reasoning proceeds by elimination. Brodie first refuses the analogy to previous
increments in destructive power. Earlier weapons could be countered by defence, by
dispersal of the target, or by counter-concentration of force; each innovation produced a
counter-innovation and the competition remained meaningful. Atomic weapons defeat this
pattern because the ratio between the effort required to deliver one and the destruction it
causes is so extreme that even a low-probability leakage through defences is catastrophic.
Interception at high rates is therefore not sufficient — a defence that stops most attackers
still permits the destruction of a country's cities.

From this follows the conclusion the work is remembered for: that the chief purpose of a
military establishment has historically been to win wars, and must henceforth be to avert
them. This is not pacifism but a claim about what force can now accomplish. Victory in the
old sense — imposing terms by destroying the opponent's capacity to resist — remains
technically available but is no longer separable from one's own destruction, because the
opponent's capacity to retaliate cannot be reliably eliminated.

The second move is about what makes averting work. Brodie argues that the relevant measure
of strength is not the size of a stockpile but whether a retaliatory capability survives an
opening attack. A country with a smaller arsenal that will certainly survive deters better
than one with a larger arsenal that might not. This is the origin of the survivability
criterion, and it is stated here more than a decade before Wohlstetter's formalisation of
it. Its practical corollary is dispersal: concentration of forces, industry and population
converts an opponent's limited stockpile into a decisive one.

He is correspondingly sceptical of numerical superiority as a strategic goal. Beyond the
threshold needed to inflict unacceptable damage, additional weapons purchase little,
because the quantity required to devastate an opponent is small relative to what either
side can build. This is an early statement of what would later be called sufficiency, and
it cuts against the arms-race logic that dominated policy in practice.

Brodie also takes seriously the possibility that the problem admits no military solution at
all, discussing international control of atomic energy as a genuine alternative rather than
a rhetorical gesture. He does not endorse it confidently, and his scepticism is grounded in
the difficulty of verification and in the absence of any enforcement mechanism that would
not itself require the weapons — but the willingness to treat the political route as a
serious competitor to the deterrent route distinguishes the work from later strategic
writing, which largely assumed the deterrent frame.

Two features date the text and matter for a 1962 persona. It predates thermonuclear
weapons, so the destructive scale it reasons about is orders of magnitude below what was
available in 1962. And it predates missiles, so it assumes delivery by aircraft with
warning times measured in hours, which materially changes the survivability problem it
poses. A Brodie persona built on this alone would understate the time pressure of a 1962
crisis; his 1959 work is the corrective.

## Claims as stated

- No defence against atomic attack can be effective enough to matter, because leakage at
  low rates is still catastrophic
- The purpose of military force shifts from winning wars to averting them
- Victory in the traditional sense is no longer separable from one's own destruction
- What deters is the survival of retaliatory capability, not the size of the arsenal
- A smaller certainly-surviving force deters better than a larger possibly-vulnerable one
- Dispersal of forces, industry and population is a strategic requirement
- Numerical superiority beyond a sufficiency threshold purchases little
- The quantity of weapons needed to devastate an opponent is small relative to producible
  stockpiles
- International control is a serious alternative, though verification and enforcement are
  unresolved

## Verify

The averting-wars formulation is closely associated with this text and should be quoted
accurately or paraphrased rather than approximated. Confirm which chapters are Brodie's and
attribute only those — the volume is edited and other contributors take different lines.
Check whether the sufficiency argument is explicit or is read back from later commentary.
Confirm the treatment of international control, which is the part most likely to be
misremembered as more dismissive than it was.

## Source

*The Absolute Weapon: Atomic Power and World Order*, Harcourt Brace, 1946. Brodie's
chapters are the ones to cite. Widely held; also available in reprint collections of
early nuclear strategy.
