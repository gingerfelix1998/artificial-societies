# Brodie 1959 — Strategy in the Missile Age

## Argument

Written at RAND thirteen years after *The Absolute Weapon*, this is Brodie's revision of his
own founding argument for a world with thermonuclear warheads and ballistic missiles. Where
the 1946 text established that force must now avert rather than win, this one asks what
follows for policy when the averting has to be done under warning times of minutes and
against destruction measured in whole societies.

Its central target is the declared doctrine of massive retaliation. Brodie's objection is
not moral but functional: a threat of general nuclear response to limited provocation is
not credible, because executing it would be irrational for the party making it, and the
opponent knows this. A threat whose execution is self-destructive does not deter the
actions it is nominally addressed to. The consequence is a gap — a class of provocations
serious enough to require response but insufficient to justify general war — and a doctrine
that leaves that gap unaddressed invites exactly the provocations that fall into it.

The prescription is capability for limited war, including the conventional forces that
massive retaliation had allowed to atrophy. This puts Brodie on the same side of the
argument as Taylor's *Uncertain Trumpet*, published the same year, though he arrives there
from strategic analysis rather than from service politics. The two together are the
intellectual basis of the flexible-response doctrine the Kennedy administration adopted.

On the balance itself, Brodie develops the survivability argument of 1946 into something
closer to Wohlstetter's contemporaneous formulation: the balance is not self-maintaining,
and the transition to missiles makes it less so, because short flight times compress the
interval in which a decision to retaliate must be taken. He is concerned with the
vulnerability of retaliatory forces and with the command arrangements that must survive
alongside them, and he treats warning and decision time as strategic variables rather than
technical details.

The work's most distinctive contribution is methodological, and it is the reason Brodie
matters as a persona distinct from the RAND analysts around him. He insists that strategy is
subordinate to policy, and that the test of a strategic concept is whether it serves a
political purpose a government could actually hold. This makes him sceptical of analyses
that optimise military outcomes in isolation — including some produced by his own
institution — and of the tendency to treat nuclear exchange as a problem in force
allocation. Strategy that cannot be connected to a political objective is not, on his view,
strategy at all.

He is accordingly unpersuaded that nuclear war can be won in any sense a government could
use, and dismissive of preventive war as a policy option, while insisting that the
capability to retaliate must be genuine rather than declaratory. He is also more attentive
than most contemporaries to the domestic and allied politics of nuclear posture — what a
doctrine communicates to publics and to allies, not only to adversaries.

For a 1962 persona this is the primary text. It postdates the missile transition, engages
the credibility problem that dominated Kennedy-era doctrine, and supplies a
policy-subordination argument that would put a Brodie persona at odds with counterforce
enthusiasm on grounds no other panel member occupies.

## Claims as stated

- Massive retaliation is not credible against limited provocation because its execution
  would be self-destructive
- An incredible threat fails to deter the actions it addresses
- There exists a class of provocations too serious to ignore and too limited to justify
  general war
- Capability for limited war, including conventional forces, is therefore required
- The balance of deterrence is not self-maintaining and the missile transition weakens it
- Warning time and decision time are strategic variables, not technical details
- Command arrangements must survive alongside the forces they control
- Strategy is subordinate to policy; a strategic concept with no attainable political
  purpose is not strategy
- Nuclear war cannot be won in any sense a government could act on
- Preventive war is not a policy option
- Nuclear posture communicates to publics and allies, not only to adversaries

## Verify

The credibility critique of massive retaliation is well attested but the specific
formulation should come from the text. Check how far he goes on limited nuclear use as
opposed to conventional limited war — this materially changes how the persona answers
escalation questions and is the same ambiguity flagged in the Taylor summary. Confirm the
policy-subordination argument's prominence; it is central to Brodie's later reputation and
may be more developed in *War and Politics* (1973), which is post-cutoff.
