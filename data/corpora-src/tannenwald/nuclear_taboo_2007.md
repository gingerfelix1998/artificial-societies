# Tannenwald 2007 — The Nuclear Taboo

## Argument

Tannenwald asks why nuclear weapons have not been used since 1945 and rejects the standard answer.
Deterrence explains restraint between nuclear-armed adversaries who fear retaliation, but not
American restraint in Korea, Vietnam or the Gulf, where the adversary had no nuclear weapons, use
was militarily contemplated, and retaliation was impossible. If restraint held where deterrence
was absent, something else is operating.

Her answer is a normative prohibition — a taboo — developed over decades, constraining choices
beyond any calculation of military utility. The argument is constructivist: the prohibition is a
social fact produced and sustained by shared understandings, shaping what decision-makers regard
as available rather than merely what they regard as wise.

The development runs through several mechanisms. World and domestic opinion made use politically
costly and leaders anticipated that cost. Non-use accumulated precedent, each instance raising the
threshold for the next. Deliberate norm-building by states and transnational movements gave the
prohibition articulation. And the character of the constraint changed over time: what began as
prudential anticipation of reputational cost became partly internalised, such that leaders came to
regard use as illegitimate rather than merely expensive.

That distinction is where Tannenwald and her critics divide. A tradition of non-use is sustained by
reputational self-deterrence — leaders refrain because they anticipate the costs of being the party
that used them. A taboo is internalised — leaders refrain because use has become unthinkable rather
than costly. The two make different predictions where reputational costs are low.

## Claims as stated

- Deterrence cannot explain non-use where the adversary had no nuclear weapons.
- Restraint held in Korea, Vietnam and the Gulf where retaliation was impossible.
- Something other than deterrence therefore constrains use.
- A normative prohibition against use developed over decades.
- The prohibition is a social fact sustained by shared understandings.
- It shapes what decision-makers regard as available, not only what they regard as wise.
- World and domestic opinion made use politically costly, and leaders anticipated the cost.
- Each instance of restraint raised the threshold for the next.
- Deliberate norm-building gave the prohibition articulation.
- The constraint shifted over time from prudential anticipation toward internalisation.
- A taboo and a tradition of non-use make different predictions where reputational costs are low.

## Verify

Distinguish carefully between Tannenwald's taboo claim and T. V. Paul's tradition claim; the
difference is the substance of any injection and the two are routinely conflated. Note that the
ExComm record already shows reputational reasoning — moral standing, being condemned as hypocrites
— which is the tradition mechanism, not the taboo. What is absent in 1962 is internalisation.
Confirm how the book treats the Cuban Missile Crisis, since a treatment of the simulated crisis
carries contamination risk.
