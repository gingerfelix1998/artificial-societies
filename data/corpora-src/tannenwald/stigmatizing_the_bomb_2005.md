# Tannenwald 2005 — Stigmatizing the Bomb

## Argument

The article that precedes and condenses the 2007 book, focused specifically on how the prohibition
came into being rather than on demonstrating that it constrains.

Tannenwald traces the stigmatisation to the immediate post-Hiroshima period and locates its
sources in three places. Public revulsion, particularly once the medical effects of radiation
became known, made the weapons categorically different in popular understanding rather than
quantitatively larger. Non-nuclear states used international institutions, especially the General
Assembly, to press for delegitimation, and their pressure gave the emerging norm formal
articulation. And within nuclear states, some officials came to hold the prohibition themselves,
which converted an external constraint into an internal one.

The mechanism she emphasises is that stigma operates by changing categories rather than by
changing costs. Once nuclear weapons were understood as a different kind of thing rather than a
larger bomb, arguments for their use had to overcome a categorical objection rather than a
cost-benefit calculation, and no military advantage answers a categorical objection.

For a 1962 panel this is more precisely dateable than the 2007 book, and its General Assembly
material is directly relevant: Resolution 1653 of November 1961 declaring nuclear use contrary to
international law falls eleven months before the crisis, with the United States among the minority
opposing. So the delegitimation process she describes was live and known at the time, while the
internalisation she argues for came later. That is the boundary an injection arm must respect.

## Claims as stated

- Stigmatisation began in the immediate post-Hiroshima period.
- Public revulsion intensified as the medical effects of radiation became known.
- Stigma made nuclear weapons categorically different rather than quantitatively larger.
- Non-nuclear states used international institutions to press for delegitimation.
- Institutional pressure gave the emerging norm formal articulation.
- Some officials within nuclear states came to hold the prohibition themselves.
- Stigma operates by changing categories rather than by changing costs.
- A categorical objection cannot be answered by military advantage.
