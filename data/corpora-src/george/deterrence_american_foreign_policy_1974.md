# George and Smoke 1974 — Deterrence in American Foreign Policy

## Argument

Written with Richard Smoke, this is the most sustained empirical attack on abstract deterrence
theory produced during the Cold War, and its method matters as much as its conclusions. Where
the RAND tradition reasoned deductively from assumptions about rational adversaries, George and
Smoke examine cases in which American deterrence actually failed and ask what the theory failed
to anticipate.

The central finding is that deterrence is not a general theory but a set of context-dependent
propositions. Whether a commitment deters depends on how the challenger reads the defender's
interests, not on the defender's capabilities or declarations considered alone. A commitment
backed by overwhelming force may fail if the challenger believes the defender's stake is small; a
weakly backed commitment may hold if the stake is believed large. Credibility is a property of
perceived interest asymmetry rather than of the threat itself, which displaces the
resolve-and-capability framing dominant in the theoretical literature.

From the cases they derive a typology of failure modes. A fait accompli presents the defender
with an accomplished fact, betting that reversal costs more than acceptance. A limited probe
tests the commitment with an action small enough to be disowned, designed to extract information
about the defender's willingness. Controlled pressure applies graduated pressure while managing
risk, calibrating each increment to stay below the level that would trigger response. Each
defeats deterrence differently, and a posture adequate against one may be useless against
another. The policy implication is that deterrence must be designed against a specific
challenger's calculus, which requires intelligence about beliefs rather than capabilities.

## Claims as stated

- Deterrence is not a general theory but a set of context-dependent propositions.
- Whether a commitment deters depends on how the challenger perceives the defender's interests.
- Credibility is a property of perceived interest asymmetry, not of the threat considered alone.
- A heavily backed commitment can fail where the challenger believes the stake is small.
- Deterrence fails in distinct modes: fait accompli, limited probe, controlled pressure.
- A posture adequate against one failure mode may be useless against another.
- Deterrence must be designed against a specific challenger's calculus, not asserted generally.
- This requires intelligence about adversary beliefs, not only about capabilities.
- Coercive diplomacy combines limited force with inducements, deadlines and assurances.
- An adversary must be given a way to comply that does not humiliate them.
