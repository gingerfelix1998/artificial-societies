# Kahn 1960 — On Thermonuclear War

## Argument

Kahn's book is the most confrontational text in the pre-1962 literature and the one whose
method, rather than whose conclusions, defines his position. Its organising move is a
refusal: he declines to treat nuclear war as an undifferentiated catastrophe about which
analysis is impossible or indecent, and insists instead on distinguishing between outcomes
that differ enormously in their consequences.

The reasoning is that deterrence policy implicitly contains claims about what happens if
deterrence fails, and those claims cannot be examined if the subject is declared
unthinkable. If a government's posture rests on the belief that any nuclear exchange is
equally and totally destructive, it will make different choices than if some exchanges
leave a recoverable society. Kahn argues the second is true — that there are states of the
postwar world which are tragic but distinguishable from one another — and that refusing to
say so does not make the difference go away, it only removes it from deliberation.

From this he derives an argument that inverts the usual relationship between war-fighting
and deterrence. If an opponent believes a country has no capability or intention to fight
and survive a nuclear war, the country's threats become less credible, because executing
them would be suicidal. Preparation for the failure of deterrence therefore strengthens
deterrence rather than undermining it. This is the logic that makes civil defence central
to his programme — shelters, industrial dispersal, post-attack recovery planning — treated
not as humanitarian provision but as a component of the deterrent.

He also distinguishes classes of deterrence by what is being deterred, and the distinction
is directly useful for a crisis panel. Deterring a direct attack on one's own territory is
one problem; deterring extreme provocations against allies or interests short of direct
attack is a harder and different one; deterring lesser provocations is harder still. A
posture adequate for the first may be useless for the second, and the credibility problem
worsens as the provocation gets smaller — which is the same gap Brodie and Taylor identified
from other directions.

The book contains the doomsday-machine thought experiment, which is frequently misread as a
proposal. Its function is analytic: a device that automatically destroys the world if
attacked would maximise deterrent threat while being obviously unacceptable, which
demonstrates that deterrent efficacy and policy acceptability are separate axes and that
maximising the first is not the objective. A Kahn persona should be able to use limiting
cases this way — reasoning from an unacceptable extreme to establish a principle — and it is
a distinctive reasoning style worth encoding.

The reception matters for the persona as much as the content. The book was widely condemned
as morally obtuse, and Kahn's response was that the moral objection is to the situation
rather than to its description. A persona modelled on him should be willing to state
conclusions others find unspeakable, and should treat squeamishness as an obstacle to
analysis rather than as evidence of anything. That disposition puts him in direct conflict
with the Ball and Robert Kennedy line in ExComm, which is a productive tension for a panel.

Note that his escalation-ladder framework — the rung-by-rung structure most associated with
his name — is from *On Escalation* (1965) and is **not** available in 1962. This is the
sharpest available injection candidate, because it lets you add a later idea from an author
already on the panel, controlling for authorial voice.

## Claims as stated

- Nuclear war is not an undifferentiated catastrophe; outcomes differ enormously
- There are postwar states that are tragic but distinguishable, and the difference matters
  for policy
- Refusing to analyse the failure of deterrence removes the question from deliberation
  without removing it from reality
- A country believed unable or unwilling to fight and survive has less credible threats
- Preparation for deterrence failure therefore strengthens deterrence
- Civil defence, dispersal and recovery planning are components of the deterrent
- Deterring direct attack, deterring extreme provocation, and deterring lesser provocation
  are distinct problems requiring different postures
- Credibility worsens as the provocation being deterred gets smaller
- Maximum deterrent threat is not the objective; efficacy and acceptability are separate
  axes
- Moral objection properly attaches to the situation, not to its analysis

## Verify

The three-type deterrence taxonomy is his and should be quoted with its actual labels and
definitions rather than paraphrased from memory. Confirm the doomsday-machine passage's
function in the text, since it is widely misrepresented. Check the civil-defence argument's
scale and specificity. Most importantly: confirm that no escalation-ladder material appears
in the 1960 book, since the pre/post cutoff distinction between this and *On Escalation* is
load-bearing for the ablation design.
