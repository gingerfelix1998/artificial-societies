# Kahn 1965 — On Escalation

## Argument

Kahn's second major work supplies the vocabulary that has organised escalation discussion ever
since, and its absence in 1962 is one of the sharpest gaps in the pre-crisis literature. *On
Thermonuclear War* had argued that nuclear outcomes differ from one another and that the
differences matter for policy. This book asks how a confrontation moves between those outcomes,
and answers with a metaphor built out into an apparatus.

The ladder is a graduated sequence of rungs from subcrisis manoeuvring at the bottom to all-out
civilian central war at the top, grouped into bands — traditional crises, intense crises,
exemplary central attacks, military central wars, civilian central wars. Between certain bands
sit thresholds: qualitative discontinuities harder to cross than ordinary rungs because both
sides recognise them as boundaries rather than increments. The no-nuclear-use threshold and the
central-sanctuary threshold are the important ones.

Two properties of the metaphor do the analytical work. Because a ladder has discrete rungs,
escalation becomes a sequence of decisions rather than a slide, which implies each step is in
principle available for deliberate choice and, crucially, that descent is possible. And because
rungs are ordered and shared, a move up is legible to the adversary as a specific quantum of
increase rather than an undifferentiated act of aggression, which makes escalation a form of
communication.

From this Kahn derives escalation dominance: the capability to escalate in ways disadvantageous
to the opponent, such that at some rung the other side would rather concede than continue. The
concept reframes crisis strength from raw capability to capability distributed across the
ladder, since a side strong at the top and weak in the middle can be squeezed where it cannot
compete. He treats escalation as a competition in risk-taking rather than in force, and
distinguishes ostensible from actual limits — those parties announce versus those they will in
fact observe — and analyses agreed battles, in which both sides tacitly accept a bounded
engagement.

## Claims as stated

- Escalation proceeds through discrete ordered rungs rather than as a continuous slide.
- Certain boundaries between rungs are thresholds, qualitatively harder to cross than ordinary
  increments.
- The no-nuclear-use and central-sanctuary thresholds are the principal discontinuities.
- Because rungs are discrete, each step is available for deliberate choice.
- Descent down the ladder is possible; escalation is not irreversible.
- A move up is legible to the adversary as a specific quantum, making escalation a form of
  communication.
- Escalation dominance, the capacity to escalate disadvantageously to the opponent, is the
  relevant measure of crisis strength.
- Capability distributed across rungs matters more than aggregate capability.
- Escalation is a competition in risk-taking rather than in force.
- Announced limits and observed limits differ, and the difference is analysable.
- Agreed battles, tacitly bounded engagements, are a stable form of limited conflict.

## Verify

The rung count and band names should come from the text; secondary accounts give varying numbers
and groupings. Confirm which thresholds Kahn names as such rather than which later commentators
identified. Confirm escalation dominance is defined here rather than attributed retrospectively.
Check how strongly he claims reversibility, since that is the claim an injection arm turns on.
